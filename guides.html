Margin vs. markup: the pricing mistake that eats profit

Markup is profit measured against cost. Margin is profit measured against price. They describe the same dollars but produce different percentages, and mixing them up is one of the most common reasons small businesses underprice.

Example: a product costs $60 and you add a 40% markup. Price = $84, profit = $24. Your margin is $24 ÷ $84 = 28.6%, not 40%. If your plan assumed a 40% margin to cover rent and salaries, you are short by more than eleven points on every sale.

To hit a target margin m, price = cost ÷ (1 − m). For a 40% margin on a $60 cost: $60 ÷ 0.60 = $100. Use the Price from Target Margin and Margin & Markup calculators.

How to estimate what an AI feature will really cost

AI model APIs charge per token — roughly four characters of English text — with separate prices for input (what you send) and output (what the model writes). Output tokens usually cost several times more than input tokens, so long answers dominate the bill.

You need four numbers: requests per day, average input tokens, average output tokens, and the provider’s current price per million tokens for each. Cost per request = (input × input price + output × output price) ÷ 1,000,000. Then multiply by daily volume.

Three things push real costs above the estimate: system prompts and retrieved documents resent on every call; retries and tool calls that multiply requests; and conversation history that grows each turn. Prompt caching, shorter outputs and smaller models for simple steps are the usual levers. Compound interest, explained with one table

Compound interest means you earn returns on your past returns. At 7% a year, $10,000 grows to about $19,700 in 10 years, $38,700 in 20 and $76,100 in 30. The last decade adds more than the first two combined — that is why starting early beats finding a slightly better rate.

The rule of 72 is a shortcut: divide 72 by the annual rate to estimate doubling time (72 ÷ 7 ≈ 10.3 years). Try your own numbers in the Compound Interest Calculator.

Break-even: the number every founder should know

Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit). The denominator is the contribution margin — what each sale contributes toward rent, salaries and software. With $12,000 monthly fixed costs, a $49 price and $19 variable cost, you need 400 sales a month before earning a profit.

Raising price by $5 cuts break-even to about 343 units; cutting variable costs by $5 does the same. Small changes in contribution margin move break-even more than most people expect. Run it in the Break-Even Calculator.

LTV : CAC — is growth actually profitable?

Customer lifetime value (LTV) is the gross profit a customer generates before leaving: monthly revenue × gross margin ÷ monthly churn. Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is what you spend in marketing and sales to win one customer. A common benchmark is LTV at least three times CAC, with CAC recovered within 12 months.

If you pay $250 to acquire a customer paying $40 a month at 75% margin with 3% monthly churn, LTV is $1,000 and the ratio is 4:1 — healthy. Double churn to 6% and LTV halves to $500, a 2:1 ratio. Churn is usually the cheapest lever to pull. Check yours with the LTV:CAC Calculator.

Mortgages: what the monthly payment hides

Early mortgage payments are mostly interest. On a $360,000 loan at 5.5% over 25 years, the principal-and-interest payment is about $2,211 a month and total interest exceeds $300,000. Property tax and insurance add to the true monthly cost.

Three levers reduce total interest: a larger down payment, a shorter amortization, and extra principal payments early in the term. The Mortgage Calculator shows the all-in payment and a balance chart.

Runway: how long until the money runs out?

Runway is cash ÷ net monthly burn (expenses minus revenue). But revenue growth changes everything: a company burning $23,000 a month today may reach break-even before running out of cash if revenue grows fast enough. That state is often called “default alive.”
